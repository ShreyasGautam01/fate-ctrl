# Causal Route Workshop — From Umbrella Dispute to Earned Escalation

**Status:** Critical review; all routes remain hypotheses.  
**Purpose:** Test whether the dramatic target is worth preserving and whether any causal chain can support it without coincidence stacking.

## 1. First challenge the ending itself

The current target combines three claims: a citywide emergency occurs; Jules becomes publicly blamed for it; and the red umbrella is visible during a live broadcast. These are not the same causal claim. The umbrella does not need to cause the emergency for Jules to be blamed, but if it merely happens to be in a picture at the climax, it is decorative rather than strategically meaningful.

Distinguish:
- **Causal responsibility:** Jules's action materially contributes to the emergency.
- **Operational responsibility:** Jules's action affects a public response, communication, or evacuation.
- **Perceived responsibility:** the public believes Jules caused or worsened the emergency.
- **Symbolic association:** the umbrella makes Jules recognizable in public footage.

The intended Fate ending may rely on perceived and symbolic responsibility rather than actual causal responsibility. If so, the game must state that distinction and give players ways to contest the attribution.

## 2. Route C1 — Local mistake, public correction window, amplified attribution

This is a refinement of the compounding-human-inference candidate, not accepted canon.

**Independent hazard:** A severe storm has already prompted a city transit authority to change service at a busy interchange. The storm is the cause of the underlying disruption; it is not created by the umbrella or Fate.

**Established public context:** A transit worker's temporary instructions and a public information display are already part of the location. The scenario must establish the exact communication arrangement before play; no unexplained public-alert system is assumed.

**Potential chain:**
1. The apartment conflict gives the umbrella and the flatmate's message thread immediate human stakes.
2. Jules leaves to return the umbrella or find a repair/transport option before the flatmate departs.
3. Jules reaches a public interchange for a reason the player can understand and influence.
4. During a service change, Jules makes a small, plausible choice—helping someone, repeating an instruction, or moving an object—that can be misinterpreted in context. The umbrella provides a visual identifier, not an authority credential.
5. A witness records a short, ambiguous moment. The recording shows what Jules did but omits a key preceding event.
6. A separate, already-established disruption causes real confusion. People infer from the clip that Jules gave or altered an instruction; that inference is plausible but contestable.
7. The public response is broadcast because the service disruption has legitimate news value. The broadcast's existence is not caused solely by Fate.
8. Fate can exploit timing, visibility, or access to existing evidence within a bounded intervention model. Players can preserve context, correct the record, reveal a source, prevent the misleading interpretation, or choose a different route.
9. If the false attribution survives a defined correction window while the real disruption escalates, Jules may become the visible scapegoat. If corrective evidence reaches the right audience in time, the blame can break or shift.

**Why this might work:** The storm causes the disruption; Jules's action affects a local response; the clip creates a plausible attribution; the umbrella identifies Jules; the broadcast makes blame public. Each link has an observable mechanism and a counterplay window.

**Why it may still fail:** It could be a generic viral-video plot. A transit instruction causing citywide harm requires more than a misleading clip; the scenario needs a believable scope for the disruption and a specific consequence beyond online outrage. The public must not leap from Jules being present to Jules causing everything without intermediate evidence.

**Hard constraint:** Do not call this a citywide emergency if the result is only embarrassment or a viral misunderstanding. Either define a credible, limited operational consequence or revise the ending's scale.

## 3. Route A1 — Misidentified civic role

A pre-existing public event uses a defined visual marker for staff or volunteers. Jules's umbrella resembles it in a limited viewpoint, and a witness misidentifies Jules in a moment of confusion.

- **Distinct mechanism:** mistaken identity based on a public convention.
- **Counterplay:** establish identity, expose the marker's actual meaning, get a legitimate staff member to correct the account.
- **Main failure risk:** a red umbrella is unlikely to be credible proof of authority; the marker convention can feel invented for the plot.
- **Reject if:** the audience must accept that people treat an umbrella as a credential without a strong, previously established reason.

## 4. Route B1 — Evidence provenance error

A real emergency report is circulated alongside a misleading image. The image's timestamp, origin, or caption is mistaken, making Jules appear connected to an instruction or event that happened elsewhere or earlier.

- **Distinct mechanism:** provenance and chronology error, not mistaken authority.
- **Counterplay:** recover the original image, compare timestamps, expose edits, establish the actual chain of custody.
- **Main failure risk:** too technical and detached from the emotional umbrella conflict; requires careful, readable evidence rules.
- **Reject if:** the player needs to understand forensic details that the game cannot communicate in one or two clear decisions.

## 6. Consider a better target: public blame during a real emergency

A potentially stronger Fate goal is:

> During a pre-existing public emergency response, make Jules the widely visible and wrongly blamed face of a consequential local failure, with the red umbrella linking Jules to the disputed event.

This preserves spectacle and moral irony while avoiding the implausible claim that a single umbrella dispute causes a citywide emergency. It may still need revision after testing.

Potential benefits:
- The emergency has an independent cause.
- The player conflict remains about truth, responsibility, trust, and evidence.
- Fate's goal is to control attribution and timing, not conjure a catastrophe.
- A non-Fate ending can allow Jules to help resolve the emergency while responsibility is correctly assigned.
- The finale can become absurd through a cascade of public interpretations and escalating institutional reactions, provided each institution's rules are introduced before use.

Potential cost:
- The ending may feel smaller than Jules causing a citywide emergency.
- The game must distinguish actual responsibility from public blame clearly.
- If public attribution is the main goal, the game risks becoming a reputation simulator unless the physical event still matters.

This is a candidate revision, not an accepted change.

## 7. What Fate is allowed to do

Potentially legal intervention classes:
- **Timing:** delay an existing correction or accelerate an already scheduled event within bounded limits.
- **Access:** make an existing piece of evidence harder or easier to reach if a causal access mechanism exists.
- **Attention:** increase the chance an existing event is observed or discussed if the audience/channel exists.
- **Route pressure:** make a known option more costly or less available without deleting player agency.
- **Interpretation pressure:** introduce a plausible but defeasible framing of evidence without changing underlying facts.

Potentially illegal intervention classes:
- invent a camera, witness, credential, or emergency after players commit;
- make a character know information they never received;
- override a settled action without a visible rule;
- force a public to believe a claim regardless of evidence;
- manufacture an emergency solely because the ending requires one.

Every intervention needs eligibility conditions, a cost/limit, an observable effect, a counterplay window, and an event explaining the change.

## 8. Milestone gate

Do not build a Fate graph until one candidate route can answer, for every edge: what must already be true; which actor causes the transition; what information the actor has; which player actions can alter it; what Fate can legally change; what evidence becomes visible; whether the route is reversible; what warning precedes a point of no return; and how the non-Fate route remains satisfying.

## 9. Recommendation

Keep the opening interpersonal conflict. Reconsider the phrase cause a citywide emergency. The strongest candidate so far is a real, independently caused public disruption whose blame is wrongly attached to Jules through incomplete evidence. Before accepting it, write an event-by-event trace and verify that every link has a human reason, an established mechanism, and a meaningful intervention.
