# Candidate Causal Routes — Red Umbrella

**Status:** Exploratory alternatives; none is canon.  
**Depends on:** SCENARIO_CAUSALITY_REVIEW.md.  
**Purpose:** Compare three causal architectures for the escalation, including their risks and counterplay. These are story-design hypotheses, not playable routes.

## Shared standard

The opening and ending are deliberately far apart in scale. The umbrella does not need magical power, but its later relevance must be more than a coincidence or a visual gimmick. Each route must establish its institutions and rules before they become consequential, preserve plausible human motivations, expose meaningful intervention points, and support a non-Fate ending.

For now, the "citywide emergency" is a target image, not a required fact. If no route can earn it, revise the ending rather than force a contrivance.

## Route A — Civic marker misclassification

### Premise
A severe weather event is independently approaching. A city preparedness exercise or public-safety operation uses visible markers to identify authorized marshals. The umbrella's red canopy resembles one such marker from a particular viewpoint, but is not itself a valid credential.

### Candidate causal chain
1. Establish an ordinary storm warning and a small, pre-existing civic preparedness event.
2. The umbrella's color and damage make it visually distinctive; its loose rib or torn canopy changes how it opens or appears.
3. Jules enters a public location for a mundane reason linked to returning the umbrella or getting it repaired.
4. A witness captures an ambiguous image that makes Jules appear to be directing or warning people.
5. Existing communication channels amplify the image, while actual officials are delayed or misidentified for independently established reasons.
6. The public narrative associates Jules with a mistaken instruction; the real storm or infrastructure problem causes the emergency.
7. Fate can influence timing, visibility, and access to evidence within defined limits; it cannot invent an official credential or force people to ignore clear contradictory evidence.

### What works
- A mundane object can become a recognizable symbol without causing a catastrophe by magic.
- The red umbrella can matter visually and as evidence.
- Counterplay exists at the image, communication, and correction stages.

### Main risks
- A red umbrella alone is weak evidence that someone is an authorized marshal.
- The chain may require too many coincidences or too much institutional incompetence.
- A public-safety exercise may feel inserted only to serve the plot.

### Proof required
The scenario must establish the marker convention, why the image is ambiguous, the specific false inference, and the independently caused emergency. At least one reasonable witness must have a reason to misinterpret the scene, and at least one credible source must be able to correct it.

## Route B — Faulty public alert relay

### Premise
A city or district has a public alert relay with a mundane failure mode already established in the scenario—for example, a manual confirmation process that can be delayed or misattributed. The umbrella becomes relevant through an ordinary chain of custody or a public demonstration, not because it has unexplained technical powers.

### Candidate causal chain
1. Establish a scheduled weather notice or civic announcement and the limited alert relay used to distribute it.
2. Establish the relay's human confirmation process and a known, bounded ambiguity (for example, two similar reports awaiting confirmation).
3. Jules carries the umbrella to a location where a report or demonstration is already scheduled.
4. A human mistakes a visible action or message associated with Jules for a confirmation signal; the umbrella is a witness-recognizable prop, not an actual control device.
5. The mistaken confirmation changes which existing message is relayed or delays the correction.
6. The independent weather/infrastructure hazard plus the delayed or incorrect public information produces a wider emergency.
7. The broadcast covers the public response; Jules is blamed because a traceable but incomplete evidence chain points to them.

### What works
- Strong information-game potential: source, confirmation, access, knowledge, and disclosure matter.
- Multiple intervention points can change whether an alert is sent, corrected, or attributed.
- Fate's legal actions could operate on timing and access rather than arbitrary plot invention.

### Main risks
- It can become an overly technical system story and displace the interpersonal opening.
- If the failure mode is not introduced early, it feels like a hidden rule revealed at the climax.
- Blaming Jules requires a specific, visible evidence chain; otherwise it is unfair rather than dramatically ironic.

### Proof required
Model the relay in plain language in no more than a few rules. Demonstrate how the flatmate/umbrella conflict plausibly intersects with it. If the link depends on Jules accidentally having privileged access, reject or redesign the route.

## Route C — Compounding human misinterpretations

### Premise
No single machine or unusual civic rule causes the crisis. Several people make understandable decisions using incomplete information. The umbrella is an identifying clue that connects events, while an independently established local emergency escalates through delayed correction and mismatched incentives.

### Candidate causal chain
1. Establish a credible local hazard and a small public event that will later be interrupted by it.
2. The umbrella dispute causes a message, photo, or meeting to be delayed, disclosed, or interpreted differently.
3. One person sees Jules with the umbrella and infers a limited, plausible fact (such as ownership or presence), not a grand conspiracy.
4. Another person combines that inference with separate but incomplete evidence.
5. A reporter or public official repeats the claim under time pressure, with visible uncertainty.
6. The real hazard escalates for reasons independent of Jules; public attention mistakenly treats the attribution as an explanation.
7. Corrections, evidence provenance, and whether characters trust each other determine if the blame persists.

### What works
- Best match for the themes of responsibility, trust, hidden knowledge, and competing interpretations.
- Supports temporary cooperation: either player can help establish the truth while still pursuing their own objective.
- The umbrella is a narrative connector, not a magical cause.

### Main risks
- The chain can become difficult to remember if too many people and facts are introduced.
- If every person makes a mistake, the story feels engineered.
- A public emergency may not plausibly produce the intended scale of blame without an additional established mechanism.

### Proof required
Keep the chain compact; every important inference must be understandable and defeasible. A player should be able to identify the earliest point where a different choice would have changed the trajectory.

## Cross-route acceptance checks

A route can proceed to a milestone sketch only if:
1. The emergency has a cause independent of Fate's desire for the ending.
2. The umbrella's relevance is established before the climax.
3. No character uses knowledge they have not acquired.
4. Every important link has a plausible alternative action or evidence-based counterplay.
5. Fate can help a route through limited, legal interventions, but cannot manufacture its prerequisites.
6. The evidence supporting blame is understandable and can be challenged.
7. At least one action by either player can change a major branch, not merely the wording.
8. The non-Fate ending resolves the interpersonal conflict rather than ending in a void.
9. The route can be explained as a short causal chain after play.
10. The absurdity comes from compounding established causes, not surprise rules.

## Current recommendation

Do not select a route yet. C is the best thematic fit on paper, but its path to a genuinely citywide emergency is currently the weakest causal link. A has the clearest visual role for the umbrella but risks contrivance. B gives strong system-level counterplay but risks losing the human story.

Next, write a one-page chain for each route with exact actors, knowledge, evidence, timing, and the point where the player can intervene. If none survives a skeptical plausibility review, revise Fate's ending to something smaller or use a different scenario.
