# Premise Discovery Screen 01 — Candidates After P2

**Date:** 2026-10-23  
**Status:** Exploratory design screen only. No premise, character, objective, Fate target, or rule is canon.  
**Inputs:** Owner's stated game direction; `SPEC.md`; `P2_RECONSTRUCTION_REVIEW_01.md`; `P2_TARGET_VIABILITY_SCREEN_01.md`.

## 1. Decision to make

P2 should not advance as the production premise merely because its radio mechanics are detailed. Its first-pass reconstruction exposed unsupported attribution, infeasible objective conjunctions, incomplete action priorities, timing mismatches and a weak route set. This screen asks a narrower question:

**Can a more immediately relatable premise create a short, story-led contest in which two players privately choose direct actions for one protagonist, while Fate pursues a measurable and counterable outcome through several genuinely different causal routes?**

The screen is not a pitch contest based on prose. A candidate survives only if its rules can make the story happen without narration inventing causes, hidden submissions leaking, or Fate deciding outcomes arbitrarily.

## 2. Hard gates for any candidate

A replacement premise must satisfy these gates before a detailed action matrix or prototype is written.

1. **Fast recognition:** the opening situation makes sense in one or two sentences, without explaining a technical system or asking players to learn a new world first.
2. **One shared protagonist:** both players submit concrete direct actions for the same person. Raw submissions stay private; only the resolved action and authorised consequences are shown.
3. **Genuine objective tension:** A and B want different things for understandable reasons. The goals must create real opportunity costs, not merely use different wording for the same desired outcome. Their success/partial-success relationship must be explicit and feasible inside the intended session.
4. **Fate is a third strategic actor:** Fate has its own observable terminal condition, bounded interventions, readable pressure and counterplay. It can inconvenience both players and sometimes make cooperation rational. It must not be a synonym for random bad luck or a hidden author forcing the ending.
5. **Three distinct causal routes:** at least three plausible routes to the same Fate milestone must differ in causal hinge, not merely in which interface, message or prop carries the same instruction. At least two credible counterplay routes must exist before the milestone is evaluated.
6. **Earned escalation:** each larger event follows from established people, objects, channels, commitments or institutions. The story can become absurd or spectacular, but it cannot jump from a tiny action to a city-scale emergency without intermediate causes and opportunities to intervene.
7. **State and information integrity:** objective predicates are measurable from authorised world state; sources prove only what they actually show; player knowledge, character knowledge, belief and narration remain separate.
8. **Short-session shape:** the concept can support a roughly 10–15 minute experience with a legible start, escalating complications and a decisive ending. The exact beat count remains open until the candidate's action economy is demonstrated.
9. **Story-first feedback:** each resolution can be narrated as a concrete event and consequence. A small number of meaningful state indicators may support the story, but objective logs and percentages cannot be the main experience.
10. **Non-Fate ending:** if Fate is stopped, the scenario still reaches a coherent, satisfying resolution rather than freezing or requiring a substitute catastrophe.

These are acceptance gates, not optional polish. Failing one means revise or reject the candidate rather than hide the gap behind additional lore.

## 3. Candidates

### Candidate A — Campus Fest Countdown

**One-sentence opening:** Twelve minutes before a student showcase opens, Jules is the volunteer holding the current stage schedule; a last-minute slot change puts one performer, one organising team and a live crowd in conflict.

**Possible conflict shape, not final objectives:** A wants the show to open on time and preserve the organising team's trust. B wants a sidelined performer to receive the promised spotlight and have the reason for the change become visible. Both can have legitimate motives; neither objective should reduce to “be good” or “make trouble.”

**Possible Fate direction, not a target:** Fate tries to turn the small schedule dispute into an unscheduled campus-wide showdown that becomes the event everyone is watching. To qualify, the terminal predicate must specify observable conditions—for example, which distinct groups arrive, what public event is actually triggered, and by what deadline. “Go viral” or “cause chaos” alone is not measurable.

**Potentially different causal mechanisms to test:**
- A public microphone/cue announcement changes what the crowd believes is happening.
- A schedule or sign-up channel redirects a separate group that is not already present.
- A physical handoff or stage/space change creates a real, independently observed second gathering.

These are only route hypotheses. If all three merely broadcast the same invitation, they count as one route. Each needs a distinct trigger, source of authority, counterplay and observable result.

**Why it is worth screening first:** The opening problem is immediately recognisable; schedule, stage, crowd and performer create concrete action targets; a small mistake can plausibly accumulate into a large public event without requiring a crime, detective structure or pre-existing emergency.

**Main risks:** The premise may still make the crowd behave like a convenient prop. The two objectives might be too compatible or one may be impossible under the time limit. A sudden “campus-wide” response could be as contrived as the old citywide-emergency ending unless each expansion step is established and counterable. It must not rely on hidden student-group beliefs that the rules never model.

### Candidate B — Community Fair Finale

**One-sentence opening:** Just before a neighbourhood fair's final event, Jules is the volunteer with the only current running order while a promised performance and the main-stage schedule compete for the same slot.

**Possible conflict shape:** One player wants the fair to finish safely and on time; the other wants a participant's promise to be honoured despite the organiser's last-minute change. Fate might try to turn the small dispute into an unplanned procession or audience-participation finale.

**Strength:** The setting and social stakes are widely readable, and physical locations, a microphone, printed schedule and stage props could support direct actions.

**Risks:** This could reproduce the same route problem as P2: several apparent actions may all be versions of “change the announcement.” A spontaneous procession may also require too many unstated crowd behaviours. Retain only if the three-route and counterplay diagrams differ mechanically and are credible without inventing audience psychology.

### Candidate C — The Wrong Birthday Delivery

**One-sentence opening:** Two deliveries arrive at the wrong doors in the same apartment block shortly before a surprise birthday; Jules has a short window to decide what to move, whom to contact and what to reveal.

**Possible conflict shape:** One player wants to preserve a private surprise and relationships; the other wants to stop an awkward mix-up from being hidden at someone else's expense. Fate might aim to turn a private surprise into an unplanned building-wide gathering.

**Strength:** Very familiar, easy to explain, and good for testing hidden intentions, disclosure and escalating social consequences.

**Risks:** Fate's larger ending risks feeling like a gimmick. The routes may collapse into different communication channels for the same reveal, and a building-wide gathering may not be dramatic enough—or sufficiently motivated—to support the desired finale. This candidate should not be stretched into a public emergency to create scale.

### Candidate D — Borrowed Radio / Operations Handover

**One-sentence opening:** During a storm-related venue disruption, Jules must choose between routing attendees, checking a faulty radio, returning borrowed equipment and dealing with an earlier warning.

**Role after review:** Keep only as a bounded mechanics micro-test if it can isolate a useful question such as action-priority completeness, hidden proposal privacy or consequence attribution. Do not keep it as the full game's premise unless it independently passes the target, objective, timing, three-route and escalation gates.

**Why it ranks lower:** It requires substantial operational explanation before the player's dilemma is felt. Its most dramatic target depends on a source-attribution claim the current evidence cannot prove, and a local queue delay does not yet support the intended finale. Adding logs, witnesses, deadlines or procedural steps to repair it would increase complexity before improving the core experience.

## 5. Next test for the leading candidate

Before writing a full scenario or changing the app, build a one-page causal skeleton for Candidate A with these fields:

1. **Opening facts:** exactly what the player sees and why Jules is responsible for the relevant objects/channels.
2. **A and B objectives:** one-sentence goals, measurable success predicates, partial outcomes, and the specific conflict over a scarce resource or deadline.
3. **Fate target:** one observable terminal predicate plus a coherent non-Fate ending. Avoid “be blamed,” “go viral,” or “cause chaos” unless the game formally defines the state that those phrases mean.
4. **Three route cards:** for each route, record initiating event → causal hinge → state change → observable consequence → evidence/source → legal counterplay → defeat condition. Collapse any two routes that share the same hinge.
5. **Fate contract:** eligibility, limited resource/cost, public signal, expiry, target evaluation time, and what Fate cannot do. The intervention must not expose a hidden submission by elimination.
6. **Beat and action feasibility:** show a plausible 10–15 minute arc. Do not require one protagonist to take two independent tasks in one action, and do not place a counteraction after its target has already been evaluated.
7. **Information boundary:** list who knows each critical fact and when; do not rely on crowd beliefs, private messages, evidence or character knowledge that has not been established.
8. **End-state examples:** at least one Fate success, one Fate defeat, and one mixed/partial player outcome. All must arise from the same rules without a narrator inventing a result.

Then run a blinded first-pass reconstruction on that skeleton before implementing it. The reviewer should receive only the packet; note any possible priming, and do not treat an AI desk review as a human playtest. Follow with a short human think-aloud session only after the rules are reconstructable.

## 6. Recommendation and decision boundary

**Recommended next experiment:** develop Candidate A into a one-page causal skeleton, with Candidate B as the fallback. This is a selection for *further investigation only*, not a canon or production-premise decision.

Keep P2 as a possible mechanics probe; do not add more P2 priority rows, evidence sources or Fate mechanics while the premise decision is open. Keep `scenarios/umbrella.json`, `scenarios/control_model_test_fixture.json`, and production UI/code unchanged. The existing prototype can later test the experience, but it cannot validate the candidate premise before the causal design is coherent.

The owner-facing gate is simple: **does the one-page candidate make sense immediately, make private direct actions strategically inferable, offer real counterplay against Fate, and earn its escalation through three distinct causal routes?** If not, revise or discard it before implementation.
