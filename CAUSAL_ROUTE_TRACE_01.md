# Causal Route Trace 01 — The Transit-Interchange Hypothesis

**Status:** Adversarial route test; explicitly not accepted as scenario canon.  
**Purpose:** Replace a vague causal outline with a checkable event chain, then determine where it fails.  
**Central question:** Can the umbrella dispute plausibly connect to a public emergency without making Jules implausibly important or letting Fate invent a crisis?

## 1. Route hypothesis

A severe storm has already caused a real transit disruption. Jules goes to a busy interchange for a mundane reason connected to returning the umbrella before the flatmate leaves. A short, incomplete recording later becomes evidence in a dispute about a local instruction during the disruption. The umbrella identifies Jules in the recording. Fate tries to make Jules the publicly blamed face of a consequential local failure.

The route does **not** claim the umbrella causes the storm or the citywide disruption. It tests whether public attribution can become the dramatic target.

## 2. Facts that must exist before play

These are proposed facts for testing, not canon:
- A storm warning and service disruption are publicly announced before Jules leaves the apartment.
- The flatmate has a real, non-arbitrary departure deadline and is traveling through the same interchange.
- Jules has a plausible reason to go there before the deadline; the route must not depend on Jules choosing the one path Fate needs.
- The interchange has official staff and an established channel for service instructions.
- A crowd, a confusing but understandable service change, and a legitimate reason for local media presence are established.
- The umbrella is visually distinctive but is not an official credential or control device.
- A recording mechanism and its limits are established before the relevant event.
- At least one trustworthy correction source exists, even if it is initially difficult to access.

If too many of these facts must be introduced solely to support this scenario, the route is overbuilt and should be rejected.

## 4. The trace's critical breakpoints

### Breakpoint A — Why is Jules there?
The flatmate's departure can motivate Jules to return the umbrella, but the story must not force the player down one corridor. A robust scenario offers at least two reasonable paths: a direct return and an alternate repair/evidence path. The public route can be one possibility rather than a mandatory funnel.

### Breakpoint B — Why does the umbrella matter?
The umbrella can identify Jules across separate observations. It cannot serve as proof of authority, proof of guilt, or a magical cause. Its narrative function is a persistent visual clue that players can protect, conceal, explain, or separate from the disputed event.

### Breakpoint C — What does the recording actually prove?
The recording can support a narrow claim about what Jules appeared to do. It cannot establish the unseen instruction, Jules's intent, or causation of the wider emergency. The information model must keep the clip, source, timestamp, omitted context, and interpretation separate.

### Breakpoint D — Why does blame reach the city?
The chain currently supports a local controversy. It does not yet justify a whole-city belief that Jules caused the emergency. A citywide audience needs a believable distribution mechanism, and the claim needs evidence that is strong enough to spread but weak enough to overturn. If that mechanism requires a long explanation, the finale will be confusing.

### Breakpoint E — What is the actual harm?
A false public accusation alone may be emotionally dramatic, but it is not a citywide emergency. If the intended game needs a physical emergency, that emergency must have an independent, legible cause and the players must influence something operationally consequential. Otherwise revise the target.

## 5. Three possible terminal targets

### Target 1 — Publicly blamed for a local operational failure
Jules becomes the visible scapegoat for a consequential failure at the interchange during a real storm disruption.

- **Causal support:** Strongest of these options.
- **Narrative scale:** Public and dramatic, but not a citywide catastrophe.
- **Counterplay:** Correct the instruction history, establish source provenance, reveal context, or prevent the misleading interpretation.
- **Current assessment:** Worth a full playable trace.

### Target 2 — Publicly blamed for worsening a wider emergency
Jules's action materially affects a local operational decision that contributes to a broader response failure, while the storm remains the independent cause of the crisis.

- **Causal support:** Possible, but needs a clear mechanism and evidence.
- **Narrative scale:** Higher stakes.
- **Counterplay:** Prevent the action, ensure the correct instruction is available, or intervene before the operational decision.
- **Current assessment:** Do not accept without a specific causal mechanism and plausible scope.

### Target 3 — Blamed for causing a citywide emergency
The public believes Jules caused the wider emergency itself.

- **Causal support:** Weak. The umbrella, an ambiguous clip, and an unrelated storm are insufficient.
- **Narrative scale:** Highest.
- **Counterplay:** Would require more extensive evidence and attribution systems, risking too much worldbuilding.
- **Current assessment:** Reject for the current scenario unless a much stronger, independently plausible mechanism is found.

## 6. Two strategy profiles to test next

These are test intentions, not prewritten outcomes.

### Profile A — Trust-first, evidence-aware
A tries to return the umbrella honestly and preserve the relationship, while avoiding unnecessary public exposure. A may still help correct a false public claim if the accusation becomes harmful.

### Profile B — Accountability-first, not sabotage-for-its-own-sake
B tries to establish the flatmate's prior knowledge and prevent Jules from accepting unfair blame. B may cooperate with A on returning the umbrella if that helps establish a trustworthy timeline.

A useful game should allow these players to conflict over timing, disclosure, and cost without making one of them irrationally hostile. If B can only succeed by sabotaging the return, revise the objective rather than treating forced opposition as depth.

## 7. Fate's intervention budget must have a causal role

The route suggests possible intervention categories, but not a selected budget:
- affect the timing of an existing opportunity;
- affect whether an already-existing source is easy to access;
- increase the chance that an existing event receives attention;
- preserve a misleading interpretation until a defined correction window closes.

Each intervention needs a cost, eligibility predicate, observable event, counterplay window, and limit. An intervention that merely says "the public believes Jules" is not a mechanic; it bypasses the evidence system.

## 8. Acceptance/rejection criteria

Promote this route only if a complete trace can show:
- every event's prerequisite and actor;
- each character's information at the moment of action;
- why Jules goes to the public location without a forced route;
- a causal role for the umbrella beyond decorative presence;
- an actual local consequence, not only social-media attention;
- a believable source and audience for public blame;
- at least two meaningful player intervention points;
- a credible correction route;
- a non-Fate ending that resolves the umbrella conflict;
- no Fate action that invents a fact or bypasses the rules.

If Target 1 is the only coherent result, change the Fate goal to Target 1. Do not retain Target 3 merely because it sounds more spectacular.

## 9. Current conclusion

This route is not ready for milestone scripting. It plausibly connects the umbrella to a public misunderstanding during an independent disruption, but does not earn the claim that Jules caused a citywide emergency. The strongest responsible next step is to test Target 1 as a compact, public, consequential failure and determine whether that provides enough spectacle. If not, the scenario premise—not the causality standard—should change.

## 10. Target 1 trial — a falsifiable playable trace

**Status:** Proposed follow-up trace, not accepted story canon or a production-ready scenario.  
**Target:** Jules becomes the public scapegoat for a dangerous crowd-routing failure at a transit interchange during a storm that already exists. The storm and transit disruption are not caused by Jules or Fate. This target tests public attribution; it does not claim Jules caused a citywide emergency.

### 10.1 Facts the scenario must establish before they matter

1. **Independent hazard:** Heavy rain has already caused water ingress and service delays at the interchange. This is shown in the opening through an existing transit alert and visible conditions.
2. **A legitimate deadline without a forced rendezvous:** Jules borrowed the flatmate's red umbrella earlier, worsened a tear, and promised to return it to the flat before the flatmate leaves for an overnight trip. Jules is on their ordinary rainy commute home, carrying the umbrella. A transit delay threatens the return deadline, but the flatmate is not artificially placed at the interchange. Jules can take an alternate route, message the flatmate, arrange a previously plausible handoff, leave the incident to staff, or choose to help. If the scenario can only bring Jules into the incident by making the flatmate meet them at this exact station, reject or replace the premise.
3. **Two imperfect information sources:** An entrance display still shows the previous route plan; a staff member is redirecting people because conditions changed. The display's timestamp and the staff member's new instruction are both observable. No hidden technical failure may be invented after a player commits.
4. **A visible operational risk:** A wet lower passage is about to close. The closure and the reason for it exist independently of Jules. Staff are already trying to prevent a crowd bottleneck.
5. **A pre-existing recording channel:** A bystander's phone records part of the crowd movement. It captures Jules near the distinctive umbrella pointing toward the passage, but its camera angle does not show the staff member's preceding signal or the stale display. The clip's actual field of view is established; no new camera or recording is conjured later.
6. **A correction source:** A staff log or full-length recording can establish the sequence of instructions and closure timing. It is not automatically available to Jules; there must be a discoverable access route and a cost in time or exposure.
7. **A bounded audience:** Local news or the transit operator's existing public channel can make the incident visible. This is a local public controversy, not a citywide broadcast magically centered on Jules.

If these setup facts feel like too much exposition for the opening, revise or replace the premise instead of adding a hidden explanation at the climax.

### 10.3 Candidate terminal outcomes

- **Fate reaches Target 1:** the local operational failure occurs; the ambiguous clip reaches a meaningful audience; and the available credible correction does not reach that audience before the scenario's declared cutoff. Jules is widely blamed, but the narration still distinguishes the real cause from the public accusation.
- **Truth breaks the attribution:** the full recording, staff log, or credible witness establishes the instruction sequence before the cutoff. The local hazard remains real; the false attribution weakens or is corrected.
- **Fate's route is avoided:** Jules takes an alternate route or stays out of the crowd. The public failure may still occur, but there is no supported chain connecting Jules to it. Fate cannot invent a clip or redirect blame after the fact.
- **Mixed human ending:** the local incident is handled and the public claim is corrected, but Jules and the flatmate do not fully repair trust—or their private conflict is resolved while the public blame remains. Partial outcomes must be explicit rather than compressed into one winner score.

### 10.4 What this trace has not solved

1. It is **one** route to local blame, not the three materially different routes required by the broader draft specification.
2. The flatmate/umbrella deadline must feel natural enough to motivate Jules's presence without forcing it. This is a likely weak link; test alternatives or replace the premise if it does not survive.
3. The player objectives need a purpose in the public incident. The current comparison fixture's narrow goals (honest return and prior-knowledge accountability) must not be assumed to provide enough strategic depth for the finale.
4. The rules need concrete action costs, deadlines, source-access rules, Fate budgets, visibility, and terminal predicates before this trace becomes executable.
5. The local-incident target may be too small for the intended over-the-top climax. If so, design a new premise that earns a larger consequence; do not relabel local public blame as a citywide emergency.

### 10.5 Decision gate

Advance this trace only if a skeptical reader can reconstruct why Jules is present, what Jules knows before each action, why the crowd-routing failure happens, exactly what the clip proves and omits, why a viewer might misinterpret it, how a correction can be found, and what actions can change the terminal result. This desk trace is not a human playtest and does not prove that the scenario is fun.

**Selection limit:** The first playable scenario should not expose all eight moves at once. Use only the moves legal in the current state and present the smallest set that offers a meaningful trade-off. Every displayed choice should say what Jules would attempt, not what objective percentage it is expected to increase.

**Direct-intention contract to preserve:** submissions are private, each player wants a protagonist action, and the resolved public event reveals what Jules actually did. The UI should not display the other player's proposal, proposal-support counts, selector ranking tuples, or other diagnostics that reveal how the action was selected. After play, a separate opt-in debrief may explain the causal chain if it does not reveal private information prematurely.
